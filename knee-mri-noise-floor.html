SETUP

The task, and the constraint that shapes everything

The competition asks for twelve binary diagnoses from a knee MRI study — ACL and meniscus tears, arthritis in three compartments, effusion, fracture, and others — scored as macro-averaged ROC-AUC. There are 4,407 training studies.

Only 58 of them carry expert image-read labels. The other 4,349 have labels that must be derived from free-text radiology reports written across nine languages. So there are two problems stacked on each other: teach a model to read knees, and first invent the labels to teach it with.

The 58 gold studies are also the only honest validation set. That is a very small number to measure against, and nearly every mistake described below traces back to forgetting it.

Why the Efficiency Track

A second prize track scores accuracy against runtime rather than accuracy alone. At the published trade ratio, 0.01 AUC is worth roughly twelve minutes of notebook time. That reframes the problem: a fast, decent model can outrank a slow, excellent one. Of the 1,184 entries on that board, 551 have a higher raw AUC than this one and still rank worse.

Discovering the noise floor

Two notebooks ran what was supposed to be the same configuration. One scored 0.8333. The control scored 0.8164. Same architecture, same data, same recipe — a gap of 0.017 from randomness alone.

That number is the most useful thing this project produced, because it retroactively demolished a lot of earlier work. Ten experiments had moved the score by less than 0.017 and been recorded as findings. They were not findings. They were coin flips written down as evidence.

Everything after this point runs as a paired test: both arms in one process, same seed, same seeded batch-order generator, so the only difference is the variable under test. A result that does not clear 0.017 is reported as inconclusive no matter how much it would be convenient to believe it.

The bug that was hiding under the null results

An ablation reversed the order of sagittal slices, expecting the medial and lateral meniscus scores to move. The measured change was 0.0021 — nothing. The conclusion recorded at the time: the architecture cannot perceive slice order.

Built on that conclusion, the next experiment added learned positional encodings to tell the model where each slice sat. It cost 0.0044. Recorded as a clean falsification. Roughly three GPU-hours spent.

Both conclusions were wrong, for the same reason. DICOM filenames are SOP Instance UIDs — assigned for uniqueness, not sequence. Every cache in the project had been built by sorting files with sorted(dir.glob("*.dcm")), which produces an order uncorrelated with anatomy. Measured across 120 sampled series:

The fix reads the geometry the headers already carry: ImageOrientationPatient gives row and column direction cosines, their cross product is the slice normal, and projecting ImagePositionPatient onto that normal gives each slice's true position along the stack. Sorting by that projection took the full-corpus rebuild from 13 minutes to 34, and was verified by a correlation check built into the rebuild itself: adjacent slices should look alike, distant ones should not.

The lesson is not "check your DICOM ordering." It is that a null result on an ablation should first raise the question of whether there was anything there to ablate.

Reversing a random sequence yields another random sequence. The evidence for the bug was sitting inside the first null result, two days before anyone read it that way.

What survived replication

Every bar below is a paired test measured against the same 58 gold studies. The shaded zone is the ±0.017 noise floor. Only results outside it mean anything, and only one of these was run twice.

The macro average also hides the most interesting result. Lateral Meniscus had been stuck between 0.665 and 0.677 across every architecture, resolution, and encoding tried. After the ordering fix it moved to 0.815 and 0.800 on the two seeds — a jump roughly nine times larger than any other target's, on the one structure most defined by its relationship to neighbouring slices.

Two later experiments then pushed it back down while gaining elsewhere. That contradiction is unresolved and is recorded as unresolved.

STANDING

Where it actually stands

The top of the efficiency board sits at 0.927–0.943 and moved several points in the week these experiments ran. An earlier estimate in this project put the target at ~0.88; that estimate was stale within days, and revising it upward was not optional.

The honest read: rank 115 of 1,184 was real for the 0.836 submission. The 0.862 resubmission should improve on that, but the efficiency board is ranked by a formula that weighs runtime as heavily as accuracy, and the public snapshot used for ranking had not caught up to this submission as of publish time — so the new rank is not yet a number I have, not a number I am choosing not to show.

METHOD

How this was built

Attribution

The implementation — training loops, preprocessing, the ordering fix, the label pipeline — was written by an AI coding agent (Claude Code) working under direction across roughly fifty notebooks. The direction, strategy, and result review were mine: choosing the Efficiency Track over the main board, deciding what to test next, reading results, and pushing back when a claim outran its evidence.

That includes catching a stale leaderboard estimate being presented as current, which is where the corrected ~0.93 target above came from. The agent-directed workflow is the honest description of the method, and it seemed worth stating plainly rather than leaving to inference.
